What are the eight trigrams? Qian, Kun, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Li, Gen and Dui explained

By Wei Chun Chuang · 2026-10-15

The eight trigrams are the most basic symbols of the I Ching. All sixty-four hexagrams are made by stacking two trigrams, so understanding the trigrams gives you the key to the whole book. This article covers their symbols, images and the usual ways to remember them.

How is a trigram built?

Each trigram has three lines, stacked from the bottom up. Every line is either yang "⚊" or yin "⚋", so there are 2×2×2 = 8 combinations: the eight trigrams. A three-line figure is called a single trigram; two stacked together form a six-line hexagram.

What does each trigram stand for?

According to the Shuogua (Discussion of the Trigrams), each has a natural image and a quality:

  • Qian ☰: three yang lines; heaven; strong and tireless.
  • Kun ☷: three yin lines; earth; yielding and receptive.
  • Zhen ☳: one yang line at the bottom; thunder; movement.
  • Xun ☴: one yin line at the bottom; wind (also wood); penetrating.
  • Kan ☵: one yang line in the middle; water; the abyss, sinking in.
  • Li ☲: one yin line in the middle; fire; clinging, brightness.
  • Gen ☶: one yang line at the top; mountain; stillness, stopping.
  • Dui ☱: one yin line at the top; lake; joy.

Is there an easy way to memorise them?

A traditional Chinese rhyme describes their shapes: Qian is three unbroken lines, Kun is broken into six; Zhen is a cup facing up, Gen a bowl turned over; Li is empty in the middle, Kan is full in the middle; Dui is broken at the top, Xun broken at the bottom. Zhen has one solid line below two broken ones, like an upturned cup; Gen is the reverse, like an upside-down bowl. Drawing each trigram while saying the rhyme makes them stick.

How do the trigrams relate to a family?

The Shuogua also describes the trigrams as a family: Qian is the father and Kun the mother; Zhen, Kan and Gen are the eldest, middle and youngest sons; Xun, Li and Dui are the eldest, middle and youngest daughters. Look at the single "odd one out" line: a yang line at the bottom is the eldest son (Zhen), in the middle the middle son (Kan), at the top the youngest son (Gen). Yin lines map to the three daughters the same way.

What is the difference between the Earlier Heaven and Later Heaven arrangements?

They are two ways of arranging the trigrams. The Earlier Heaven (Fuxi) sequence runs Qian 1, Dui 2, Li 3, Zhen 4, Xun 5, Kan 6, Gen 7, Kun 8; in a circle Qian sits south and Kun north, with each trigram facing its opposite. The Later Heaven (King Wen) arrangement places Li south, Kan north, Zhen east and Dui west, with Qian northwest, Kun southwest, Gen northeast and Xun southeast. For reading the classic, learn the trigrams' images first; the arrangements can wait.

What comes after learning the trigrams?

Practise splitting the sixty-four hexagrams into upper and lower trigrams. Heaven over earth is Obstruction (Pi); earth over heaven is Peace (Tai). Once you can take any hexagram apart at a glance, you are ready to read the judgments and line statements.

FAQ

Is the order of the eight trigrams fixed?
The eight symbols are fixed, but there is more than one arrangement. The Earlier Heaven and Later Heaven arrangements are the most common, with different orders, directions and uses.
Does Xun mean wind or wood?
Both. The Shuogua gives Xun as wind and also as wood; which image applies depends on context.
How do the trigrams relate to the sixty-four hexagrams?
Each hexagram is two trigrams stacked, so eight times eight gives sixty-four. The bottom three lines form the lower trigram and the top three the upper trigram.
