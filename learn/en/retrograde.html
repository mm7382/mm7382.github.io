What does retrograde actually mean?

By Wei Chun Chuang · 2026-10-24

Mercury retrograde is probably the most-mentioned and most-misunderstood term in astrology. This article explains the astronomy first, then describes separately how astrological practice reads a retrograde planet, and finally states plainly that no established causal link joins the two.

Planets do not move backwards

The most important sentence first: a planet never reverses its orbit. Planets keep travelling the same way round the Sun. They do not stop, and they do not turn around.

Retrograde motion is apparent motion, that is, the direction we see from Earth. We observe one moving planet while standing on another moving planet, so all we see is their relative position. When the direction of that relative change flips, the planet appears for a while to track backwards against the background stars. That is retrograde.

Why does it look like it goes back?

Because the two planets orbit at different speeds. The closer to the Sun, the faster the orbit.

  1. Outer planets (Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and so on) orbit more slowly than Earth. As Earth overtakes one from the inside, that planet appears to fall backwards for a time. It is like passing a slow car on a highway: against distant hills, the car seems to slide backwards.
  2. Inner planets (Mercury, Venus) orbit faster than Earth. When one of them overtakes Earth from the inside, the same reversal of relative direction occurs.

The mechanism is the same in both cases: a visual reversal produced by relative orbital speeds, not a change in how the planet is actually moving.

How often does Mercury go retrograde?

Mercury is closest to the Sun and orbits fastest, so it catches up with Earth most often. It appears retrograde roughly three to four times a year, for about three weeks each time. Venus, by comparison, does so about once every two years, Mars about every two years, and the outer planets each have a longer retrograde stretch every year.

For exact dates, consult an ephemeris or work them out from NASA's public planetary position data.

What are the station points?

Retrograde motion does not begin abruptly. Before and after the turn, the planet's apparent motion slows until, for a few days, it seems to sit at nearly the same degree. That is a station. The moment before direct motion becomes retrograde is the retrograde station; the moment before it resumes forward motion is the direct station. The start and end dates printed in an ephemeris are those two station points.

How does astrological practice read it?

In the astrological tradition a retrograde planet is generally not treated as broken or disabled, but as the same planet turned inward or revisited: a function that normally expresses outward is instead reviewing, sorting and revising. So the usual reading is re-reading, re-negotiating and redoing, not being unable to act.

A planet retrograde in a natal chart is described as a long-standing disposition, while a current retrograde period in the sky is treated in practice as a stretch suited to review and to finishing things off. Those are two different levels of statement.

About effects, to be clear

This has to be said straight: there is no established physical mechanism by which a planet's apparent retrograde motion affects electronics, travel, flights or communication. Attributing a delayed contract, a dead phone or a misread message to Mercury retrograde is a narrative applied after the fact, not a testable cause.

So this article keeps the two apart. The astronomy is observable, calculable and verifiable. The astrological practice is an inherited language of interpretation, which this site introduces as a tool for reflection and self-observation, with no claim that it predicts events. Plan important trips, contracts, medical or financial decisions on ordinary practical grounds and on professional advice, not around a retrograde period.

FAQ

Does a planet really move backwards when retrograde?
No. A planet keeps orbiting the Sun in the same direction throughout. Retrograde motion is an appearance from Earth: because the two planets orbit at different speeds, their relative positions shift and the planet seems to track backwards against the background stars.
How often is Mercury retrograde, and for how long?
Roughly three to four times a year, for about three weeks each time. For a few days before and after, the planet appears almost motionless; those are the station points. Exact dates come from an ephemeris or from NASA's public planetary position data.
