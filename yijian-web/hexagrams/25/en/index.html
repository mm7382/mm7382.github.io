Wu Wang · Innocence

Act from an honest motive and accept what you cannot control.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Wu Wang: Supreme success. Steadfast correctness is beneficial. What is not right brings calamity; proceeding is unfavorable.

The Tuan commentary reads this as firmness arriving from outside and taking charge within: movement that is strong, great success through correctness. The Xiang image is thunder rolling under heaven, each thing growing true to its own nature, and rulers nourishing all things in step with the season.

Qian above, Zhen below. Thunder moves across the land and everything grows according to what it actually is, without contrivance. The judgement grants the four virtues of sublime success through correctness, then immediately adds a warning: stray from an honest motive and you invite fault. The lines cover both sides of this. Some speak of solid work done without counting the harvest; others speak of trouble you did not cause at all. You can hold your own conduct steady. You cannot hold every outcome.

The judgment contains the four virtues of origination, success, rightness, and steadfastness, with a positive overall direction, hence the rating: Favorable. Yet “what is not right brings calamity” and the top line's “action brings calamity; nothing beneficial” explicitly warn against flawed motives or forcing forward, so it is not the highest rating. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: act on the facts and through proper channels, and adjust when something unexpected lands on you. Avoid: reading the situation through your own wishes, or trying to force control over every result.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Qian above, Zhen below: movement underneath, firmness above — a structure without pretence.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Wu Wang.
  • Say what “without pretence” means structurally.
  • Work out that Without Pretence with its halves swapped is Great Strength.

The lower trigram of Wu Wang is Zhen: a solid first line with two broken above. The upper is Qian, all solid. The first, fourth, fifth and sixth lines are solid. The movement is in the lowest line, with a whole Qian above.

Wang means false or forced; wu wang is not forcing. The stirring is in the lowest line and a whole Qian stands above, so nothing in the structure is taking a detour. It is often glossed as going along with nature, but more precisely it means adding nothing extra — moving on the conditions as they are, without calculation layered on top. That promises no outcome; it only says the process involves no pretence.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a proposal that states its risks as they are, neither polished nor inflated. Wu Wang describes that structure of adding nothing; it does not mean the proposal is accepted.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Nine at the beginning: Innocence. Going forward brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says that going forward without pretence lets the will find its range. Because the starting point is honest, the line is read as favourable movement.

You set out from a state that is neither false nor contrived. The motive is clean, so your intent can extend itself, and the line reads well.

At the start of something, ask yourself plainly why you are doing it. When the reason is clear and carries no performance or calculation, the action stands on firmer ground.

What is the most honest reason you are pushing this forward?

Six in the second place: Not plowing for the harvest, nor clearing new land for cultivated fields; then it is beneficial to proceed.

The Xiang commentary notes that not reaping while ploughing means wealth is not the point. You do not tally the harvest while sowing, nor expect rich fields while clearing rough ground.

Do not count the yield while the seed is still going in. Put your attention on the work in front of you rather than on the return you have already imagined. That is what makes going forward suitable.

In work or study, make the current step solid instead of re-estimating results that have not happened yet. Daily recalculation eats the hours the work needs.

How much of your energy goes into the work itself, and how much into predicting the payoff?

Six in the third place: An undeserved calamity. A tethered ox is a traveler's gain and the villagers' misfortune.

An ox is tethered by the road. A passing traveller leads it away, and the townspeople carry the suspicion and the loss. The Xiang commentary marks this as trouble arriving through no fault of your own.

The traveller gains, the locals pay. This is the situation where you did nothing wrong and are caught in it anyway. Undeserved misfortune is real and the text says so plainly.

Some losses come from other people's behaviour or from a gap in the system. Work out where responsibility actually sits and keep records that show it, rather than starting with self-blame.

In this loss, what did you actually cause, and what were you simply caught by?

Nine in the fourth place: One may remain steadfast. No blame.

The Xiang commentary explains that steadfastness without fault comes from holding what is already yours. You keep to your own correct ground and make no extravagant claim outward.

You can hold firm, and there is no fault in it. The point is not to gain more but to keep a steady grip on the principles and the position that are properly yours.

When things shift around you and you are unsure whether to follow, keep your existing rhythm and standards first. Do not move your own limits to match whichever way the wind turns.

Which of your principles do you want to keep no matter how the situation changes?

Nine in the fifth place: An undeserved illness. Without medicine, there is joy.

The Xiang commentary warns that medicine for an affliction not of your own making should not be tried. Reaching for a remedy can itself create the trouble. This is read as a textual image, not as health advice.

An ailment you did not bring on yourself does not need an urgent intervention. Letting it settle is the better course here. The medicine in the original is a figure of speech, not a medical instruction.

When a small setback was not caused by you, watching and waiting is often the right handling. Adding activity just to look like you are dealing with it tends to make more work.

Which of your recent fixes created more trouble than the original problem?

Nine at the top: Innocence, yet action brings calamity. Nothing is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary calls this the misfortune of exhaustion: movement at the final position, when the situation has already run to its limit, brings its own trouble.

Even with no false intent, pushing on after the momentum is spent produces fault and brings no advantage. When the timing is finished, the right move is to stop.

Once something has been taken as far as the conditions allow, withdrawing suits better than raising the stake. A good motive does not substitute for reading the timing.

Are you continuing because the conditions still hold, or because you do not want to admit it is over?

Act according to the existing conditions without adding anything artificial (Correct) : Correct. It concerns an honest process, not guaranteed results.

Doing nothing at all : Lower Zhen means movement, so this hexagram includes action.

A guarantee that nothing goes wrong : Hexagrams describe structures and do not guarantee outcomes.
