Xun · The Gentle

Enter by seeping in, state your case clearly, and keep your own ground.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Xun: Small success. It is beneficial to proceed and beneficial to meet a great person.

The Tuan commentary reads doubled Xun as an order stated and stated again: strength that settles gently into a central, correct place, so the intent can actually travel. Because the yielding lines all follow the firm ones, success here is small. The Xiang image is wind following wind, with the noble person repeating the instruction before acting on it.

Xun above, Xun below. Wind upon wind, and the sense of the hexagram is entering by yielding. Wind gets everywhere not by force but by seeping in. The judgement grants only small success: the road is passable but its range is limited, and it still favours having somewhere to go and seeing the person who can decide. The lines run from hesitation at the threshold, through excessive self-lowering that still works because the heart stays centred, to careful reform announced before and tracked after, and finally to yielding so far that you lose your own means. Gentleness is a method of entry, not a reason to give yourself up.

The judgment limits success to small progress, unlike hexagrams of great success; beneficial proceeding and meeting a great person add positive conditions. The lines are evenly mixed: the second nine's fortune without blame, fourth six's vanished regret and three kinds of game, and fifth nine's steadfast fortune with nothing unfavorable are clearly positive. The first's advance and retreat reveals doubt, the third's repeated submission exhausted purpose, and the top's lost resources and axe with misfortune is the strongest warning. Opportunity and excessive compliance coexist; judgment of limits determines gain and loss, hence the rating: Neutral. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: communicate clearly and repeatedly, advance by soft pressure, and keep your own view intact. Avoid: restating things endlessly without a point, or yielding until you no longer hold any power to decide.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Xun upon Xun: two yieldings stacked, each broken line at the base of its half.

  • Recognise the hexagram whose two halves are both Xun.
  • Explain why the force of Xun is at the very bottom.
  • Work out that The Gentle with its first line flipped is Small Restraint.

Both halves of Xun are Xun: in each, the lowest line is broken and the two above are solid. The broken lines of the figure are the first and fourth. The halves are identical, so this hexagram too is studied by flipping one line.

Xun is wind, and also entering. Each broken line sits at the base of its half, so the yielding end is at the bottom — wind does not press down from above; it works in through a gap. This hexagram is about entering somewhere that does not welcome you: not by force, but by finding the gap at the very bottom.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a new practice starts with one team willing to try it rather than with persuading management. Xun describes that structure of entering from below.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: Advancing and retreating. A warrior's steadfastness is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary says that advancing and retreating shows a doubting will, and that the steadfastness of a soldier is favourable because it brings that wavering will under order.

You stand at the door, half in and half out, unable to settle. What suits here is the soldier's kind of correctness: plain, decisive, and enough to shut the wavering down.

Excessive caution in a new environment leaves you stuck where you are. Set one simple rule for yourself and follow it a few times; acting clears hesitation faster than more thinking does.

After days of turning this over, is what you lack information or resolve?

Nine in the second place: Submission beneath the bed. Employ scribes and shamans in great numbers. Good fortune and no blame.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune of this repeated appeal to holding the central position. The behaviour is lower than it needs to be, but the heart still keeps its measure.

You bow so low it is as if you were under the bed, and you keep going through intermediaries to make yourself heard. It looks excessive, yet because you hold your own measure inside, the outcome is favourable and without fault.

Lowering your posture in a forceful environment, and asking someone to relay for you, is not humiliating. As long as you know what you are there to win, a low stance is a tactic rather than a surrender.

When you lower your stance, is your own limit still in place?

Nine in the third place: Repeated submission. Regret.

The Xiang commentary says the regret of repeated submission comes from a will that has run dry: there is nothing left to put forward as your own.

You give way again and again until the position becomes uncomfortable. The trouble is not the bowing itself but that there is no longer any view of your own behind it.

Accommodate people long enough and you lose the ability to say what you want. Next time you are asked to concede, state your own view first, then decide whether to concede.

The last time you went along with someone, was it agreement or just fatigue?

Six in the fourth place: Regret vanishes. Three kinds of game taken in the hunt.

The Xiang commentary closes with the word merit: the hunt that yields three kinds of game is the line where something real is achieved. The three kinds are enough for sacrifice, for guests and for the kitchen.

Earlier regret disappears, and a single hunt brings back three kinds of game, enough to serve several purposes. This is the rare line in the hexagram where actual results arrive.

People willing to go in low and work a job through from the start usually come away with several things at once: experience, contacts and reputation. Do not count only the most obvious return.

Besides money, what did the last thing you finished actually give you?

Nine in the fifth place: Steadfastness brings good fortune, regret vanishes, and nothing is unfavorable. No beginning, but an ending. Three days before geng and three days after geng. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune here to a position both correct and central. The three days before and after the day of change stand for full explanation beforehand and steady follow-up afterwards.

Holding correct brings good fortune, regret disappears, and nothing is unfavourable. The start is rough but the finish is sound. When you change a rule, explain it well in advance and keep watching how it lands afterwards.

Do not spring a new process on people. Let them hear why it is changing before it changes, then go back and check how it is working. Resistance drops sharply when you do both.

Have the people affected by your change actually understood the reason for it?

Nine at the top: Submission beneath the bed, losing resources and an axe. Persistence brings misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says bowing under the bed at the top position shows that the way has run out, and that losing your goods and axe is dangerous even if you hold to it. The axe stands for the means by which you earn and decide.

At the highest place you press yourself under the bed again, and you lose the very things you live and decide by. Holding to that position without changing it is where the danger lies.

Concede far enough and you hand over your expertise and your say along with everything else. That is no longer modesty. The signal to stop is the moment you start thinking it makes no difference whether you are there.

To keep the peace, have you given away something you should have kept?

Quick Quiz

Which two lines of Xun are broken?
  • The first and the fourth
  • The third and the sixth
  • The second and the fifth
View answers and explanations for every option

The first and fourth lines (Correct) : Correct. Each Xun trigram has a broken bottom line.

The third and the sixth : A broken top line in each group forms doubled Dui, Hexagram 58.

The second and the fifth : A broken middle line in each group forms doubled Li, Hexagram 30.

How does Xun enter?
  • Through the gap at the bottom, not by force
  • By pressing down from above
  • By cutting in at the middle
View answers and explanations for every option

Entering through the lowest gap without relying on force (Correct) : Correct. Each group has its broken line at the bottom.

By pressing down from above : The upper two lines are solid; the yielding end is at the base.

By cutting in at the middle : The second and fifth lines are solid.

The first line of Xun flips to yang. Which hexagram results?
  • Xiao Xu · Small Accumulation
  • Gou · Coming to Meet
  • Jiā Rén · The Household
View answers and explanations for every option

Xiao Xu · Small Accumulation (Correct) : Correct. Lower Xun changes to Qian while upper Xun remains, forming Hexagram 9, Xiao Xu.

Gou · Coming to Meet : Gou has Xun below and Qian above; its upper trigram is not Xun.

Jiā Rén · The Household : Jia Ren has Li below and Xun above; its lower trigram is not Qian.
