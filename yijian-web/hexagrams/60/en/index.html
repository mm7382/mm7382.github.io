Jie · Limitation

Set the limit where you can hold it; too loose spills, too tight snaps.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Jie: Success. Bitter limitation cannot be maintained steadfastly.

The Tuan commentary grounds the success of Jie in firm and yielding each keeping their share, with firmness at the centre, and says bitter limitation cannot be held because that road has run out. Heaven and earth have their limits and so the four seasons come about; limit by institution and you neither damage wealth nor harm people. The Xiang image is water above the lake, with the noble person setting measures and weighing conduct.

Kan above, Dui below. Water stands over the lake, and the hexagram is about limits. The character means the joint of a bamboo: one clear segment after another. A lake holds water, but only so much, and past that it spills. The judgement says limitation brings success, then immediately adds that bitter limitation cannot be held to. The lines run from staying inside the courtyard because the way is blocked, to failing to go out when the way is open and suffering for it, to regret with nobody to blame, to limits that feel settled, to limits so well judged they taste sweet, and finally to limitation pushed into pain. Set the limit where you can hold it for a long time: too loose and it spills, too tight and it snaps.

The judgment gives success, then limits it by warning against bitter restriction. The lines likewise span both ends: the fourth six's peaceful limitation succeeds, and the fifth nine's sweet limitation brings fortune and worthwhile proceeding. The first and third have no blame. Yet the second nine's staying behind the gate brings misfortune through lost timing, and the top's bitter restriction brings misfortune because its way is exhausted. Fortune depends entirely on appropriate measure: remaining indoors is blameless in the first line and unfortunate in the second, differing only in timing, hence the rating: Neutral. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: set boundaries you can keep for the long run, and give your time and resources a clear measure. Avoid: rules so loose that things get away from you, or restrictions so severe that nobody can sustain them.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Kan above, Dui below: water over a lake — it holds because there is a boundary.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Jie (Limitation).
  • Explain why limitation is positive here.
  • Say that swapping the halves turns Limitation into Confinement.

The lower trigram of Jie is Dui: two solid lines with a broken third. The upper is Kan: broken, solid, broken. The first, second and fifth lines are solid.

Dui is a lake, and a lake is a lake because it has banks. However much water there is, without a boundary it is not a lake. This hexagram concerns restraint: a limit is not a reduction but what lets something be kept. The difficulty is in the amount — too loose and it does not hold, too tight and it does not either, which is why tradition stresses a limitation one can live with rather than a harsh one.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a team caps work in progress at three items, not to do less but to finish what it starts. Jie describes that structure.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Nine at the beginning: Not leaving the inner doorway and courtyard. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says that not leaving the inner courtyard is a matter of knowing what is open and what is blocked. The courtyard here is the inner one, closer in than the outer gate.

You do not even step out into your own inner courtyard, and no fault attaches, because you know clearly whether this is a time that is open or a time that is shut.

Holding still when the timing is wrong is judgement rather than idleness. The test is whether you can say what you are waiting for, or are simply afraid to move.

Is your holding still a judgement or an avoidance?

Nine in the second place: Not leaving the outer gate and courtyard. Misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says that not going out of the outer courtyard misses the moment utterly. The act is the same as in the first line; the difference is that the gate is now open and still not used.

Again you do not go out, and this time it goes badly. The gate could be walked through and you stay inside, so the moment is missed completely.

Caution stops protecting you and starts costing you. When the conditions around you are all in place and you are still finding reasons to delay, that is not steadiness but a miss.

Is there an opening where everything is ready and you are still waiting?

Six in the third place: Without restraint, there is sighing. No blame.

The Xiang commentary asks who else there is to blame for the sighing that follows a lack of limits. The absence of fault means the fault cannot be laid on anyone else.

You will not set a limit, and sighing is what is left. The text says no fault, and the commentary makes the sense blunt: there is nobody else to blame for this.

The regret after a late night, an overspend or an overstated promise usually has no other author. Admitting that is unpleasant and it is the only place a change can start.

How much of your latest regret came from your own indulgence?

Six in the fourth place: Peaceful limitation. Success.

The Xiang commentary says the success of settled limitation comes from following the correct way already laid down above. The measure is not something forced into place by your own effort.

You sit comfortably inside the limit, and it goes well. The reason given is that you are following an established correct way rather than gritting your teeth to hold a line of your own.

A good limit should feel steady rather than like a daily fight with yourself. If a rule takes fresh resistance every day to obey, it is probably cut wrong for you.

Do the rules you set for yourself leave you settled or exhausted?

Nine in the fifth place: Sweet limitation. Good fortune; proceeding has merit.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune of sweet limitation to holding the central position. A measure that stays central is not bitter but sweet.

The limit is judged so well that it is actually pleasant, which brings good fortune, and going forward earns esteem. Everything rests on staying central.

The best self-discipline is the kind you enjoy: eating about right, spending about right, working about right. Reach that level and other people copy you without being told.

Is there a good habit of yours that you genuinely enjoy?

Six at the top: Bitter limitation. Persistence brings misfortune; regret vanishes.

The Xiang commentary says bitter limitation held to is dangerous because the way has run out. Yet the text also says regret disappears, so bearing the hardship willingly at least leaves nothing to regret.

Limitation pushed to the point of pain is dangerous if you keep holding it, because the road ends there. The text still says regret disappears: hardship accepted willingly leaves no remorse behind.

Economising or disciplining yourself to the edge of what you can bear does not last. If this is a stretch you genuinely have to walk, at least put an end date on it.

The thing you are gritting through now, until when do you plan to keep it up?

Quick Quiz

Why is the limitation in Jie positive?
  • A boundary is what lets something be kept
  • Because a limit means doing less
  • Because tighter is better
View answers and explanations for every option

Boundaries make containment possible (Correct) : Correct. Dui is a lake; its banks make it a lake.

Because a limit means doing less : The hexagram concerns retention, not reduction.

Because tighter is better : Overly tight boundaries also fail to contain; the challenge is the measure.

Which trigram is the upper half of Jie?
  • Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle)
  • Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above)
  • Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above)
View answers and explanations for every option

Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle) (Correct) : Correct. Broken fourth, solid fifth, broken sixth.

Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above) : Dui is Jie's lower trigram.

Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above) : Jie's sixth line is broken.

Where is the difficulty in Jie?
  • In the amount: too loose or too tight both fail
  • In having no limit at all
  • In there being too much water
View answers and explanations for every option

The measure: too loose or too tight, it cannot hold (Correct) : Correct. Hence the tradition emphasizes sustainable restraint.

In having no limit at all : That is precisely what the hexagram addresses.

In there being too much water : Abundant water is not the issue; absent banks are.
