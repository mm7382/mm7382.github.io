Fu · Return

Turn back early, then restart at a pace you can keep.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Fu: Success. Coming and going without affliction; friends arrive without blame. Returning along the path, return comes in seven days. It is beneficial to have somewhere to go.

The Tuan commentary says the firm line returns and moves in accord, which is why coming and going bring no trouble; the cycle has its own timing. The Xiang commentary shows thunder within the earth and early kings closing the passes at the solstice, since new life needs quiet rather than driving.

Thunder lies within the earth and a single firm line is born beneath five yielding ones: life returning after the stripping away. The judgement says coming and going bring no trouble and friends arrive without blame, and the seven-day return marks a cycle that runs to its own timing and cannot be hurried. All six lines are about how you come back: returning before going far, returning gracefully, returning again and again, returning alone, returning with substance, and at the top losing the way entirely. What decides this is whether you notice the drift early, and whether the first shoot is given cover.

“Success,” “no blame,” and “beneficial to proceed,” reinforced by supreme good fortune in the first nine, clearly favor turning back and correcting course. Yet repeated return in the third six carries danger, while confused return at the top brings misfortune and a warning of great defeat. Repetition or stubborn refusal to return can reverse the outcome, hence the rating: Highly Favorable, rather than the highest. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: get back on course early and restart with one small step you can sustain. Avoid: delaying the correction out of embarrassment, or pushing for results the moment things start to recover.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Kun above, Zhen below: the one solid line is at the very bottom — a single yang just returned.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Fu.
  • Contrast Fu and Bo structurally.
  • Say that swapping the halves turns Returning into Enthusiasm.

The lower trigram of Fu is Zhen: a solid first line with two broken above. The upper is Kun, all broken. Only the first line of the figure is solid. It is the counterpart of Bo: Bo retains one solid line at the top, while in Fu one has just appeared at the bottom.

One solid line at the very bottom with five yin lines above reads structurally as a return that has only just begun: the direction has changed, the quantity is still tiny. This hexagram is most often misread as “things improve”. What it states is a starting point, not an outcome — and because the start is small, whether it grows is not what this figure addresses.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a long-stalled project again has one person steadily pushing it, with nothing else restored yet. Fu describes that structure of a return just begun; it does not say the project gets back on track.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Nine at the beginning: Returning before going far. No cause for regret. Supreme good fortune.

Returning before going far, so no deep regret is left; the line is judged highly fortunate. The Xiang commentary treats this as the work of self-cultivation.

You turned back before straying far, so no heavy regret remains. Catching a mistake while it is still small costs the least of any option here.

The moment you see your approach is wrong, adjust, even if it means admitting a misjudgement. The earlier you turn back, the less there is to repair.

At which step did the road you are on start to bend away?

Six in the second place: Restful return. Good fortune.

A fine, unhurried return, and therefore fortunate. The Xiang commentary explains it by the willingness to be humble and stay close to people of character.

You come back to the straight path calmly and well, which goes fortunately. The reason is closeness to people of character, and good influence makes turning back feel natural rather than forced.

When changing a habit, getting near people who do it well and joining a healthy setting usually works better than willpower alone, and it lasts longer.

Whose way of living around you is the one you would want to move closer to?

Six in the third place: Repeated return. Danger, but no blame.

Returning repeatedly: there is danger, but no blame. The Xiang commentary affirms that in principle the one who keeps coming back is not at fault; the risk lies in the repetition, not in the return.

You drift and return, drift and return, and the repetition itself carries risk. Still, the line clears you of fault: what is dangerous is the relapsing, not the coming back.

Breaking an old habit or rebuilding a routine usually involves repeated failure. What counts is being willing to start again after each break, while looking at which situation keeps pulling you back.

Which recurring situation keeps returning you to the old pattern?

Six in the fourth place: Walking among others, returning alone.

Walking in the midst of others and returning alone. The Xiang commentary says this follows reason rather than numbers; turning alone takes judgement and the capacity to be misunderstood.

You are among others and the only one who turns back. That follows reason rather than numbers, and it takes both judgement and the willingness not to be understood.

When everyone around you carries on as before, being the first to change direction feels lonely. Explain your reasons clearly and accept that not everyone will follow.

If nobody turns with you, can you still keep going?

Six in the fifth place: Generous return. No regret.

A substantial, generous return, and so no regret. The Xiang commentary places the key in holding the centre and examining oneself, rather than waiting to be corrected by others.

You come back to the straight path in a solid, unshowy way, so nothing is left to regret. The key is staying centred and checking yourself instead of waiting for someone else to point it out.

Build a habit of regular self-review, such as looking over your progress and condition once a week, so correction becomes routine rather than something you improvise after a failure.

When did you last seriously review the direction you are going in?

Six at the top: Confused return. Misfortune, calamity, and error. Mobilizing an army ends in great defeat, with misfortune for the ruler. For ten years, unable to campaign.

Losing the way and still not returning: the most unfortunate line of the hexagram, carrying calamity. It speaks of an army defeated and ten years unable to march again; the Xiang commentary traces it to going against the straight path.

You have lost the way and still will not turn back, which is the worst place in this hexagram. The image of a defeated army and ten years without a campaign shows how long the cost lasts. Persisting takes a very long time to repair.

When every signal says the direction is wrong, putting more in stretches the loss out for years. Stop and reassess, and put withdrawing or changing route on the list of options.

Are you still adjusting, or have you stopped admitting the direction is wrong?
