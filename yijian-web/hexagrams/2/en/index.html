Kun · The Receptive

Support all things with generous virtue, respond to circumstances, and find good fortune through quiet steadfastness

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Kun: Supreme success. The mare's steadfastness is beneficial. The noble person has somewhere to go: leading brings confusion, following finds a guide. Beneficial to gain friends in the southwest and lose friends in the northeast. Peaceful steadfastness brings good fortune.

Commentary on the Judgment: “Perfect indeed is Kun's originating power! All things draw life from it, as it receptively carries Heaven.” Commentary on the Image: “Earth's disposition is receptive; the noble person therefore supports all things with generous virtue.” (Zhouyi, Commentaries on the Judgment and the Image; public domain)

Kun is entirely yin, symbolizing Earth's power to support all things. It emphasizes receiving, inclusion, and steadfastness rather than initiating. Like a mare's constancy, it is receptive yet strong, flexible yet enduring. Follow the larger movement of circumstances and treat others generously rather than advancing rashly on your own. Quiet correctness brings good fortune.

Kun offers supreme success, and “peaceful steadfastness brings good fortune” explicitly indicates a positive direction. Earth supports all things with generous virtue and is generally auspicious. Yet it requires correctness, with slightly less initiating force than pure-yang Qian, hence the rating: Highly Favorable, rather than Highest Favorability.

Do: Accept present conditions first, cooperate steadily, and care for the foundations. Avoid: Seeking prominence before conditions mature or losing principles through passivity.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Six yin lines: both halves are Kun, and not one line is solid.

  • Recognise that the hexagram of six yin lines is Kun.
  • State how Kun and Qian are related structurally.
  • Say that flipping the first line of The Receptive gives Returning.

Kun is Kun stacked on Kun: the lower three lines are broken and so are the upper three. It is the line-by-line opposite of Qian — wherever Qian has a solid line, Kun has a broken one. Its two halves are identical, so Kun too is observed by flipping a single line.

Kun stands for earth, and for carrying, holding and going along with things. Six yin lines do not mean emptiness; the whole hexagram describes a structure of receiving and accommodating. It is often called passive, but a more accurate reading is that the force here sits at the receiving end rather than the initiating one. Without something to receive it, initiating force goes nowhere.

A teaching example, not a prediction: the person on a team who absorbs whatever arrives: requests get scheduled, resources get allocated, and they propose no new direction of their own. Kun describes the structure of that position. It does not say such a person is lesser, nor does it say how the team will fare.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: Treading on frost; solid ice is coming.

Commentary on the Image: “Treading on frost, then solid ice: yin begins to congeal. Following its course gradually brings solid ice.” Yin grows gradually; be alert early and prevent small problems from becoming large. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Walking on thin frost signals solid ice ahead. A beginning already contains clues to its direction; notice and respond early.

If small problems already show signs, address them now rather than waiting until they become serious. Prevent their growth at the start.

Are small problems you have overlooked accumulating? What was the earliest warning signal?

Six in the second place: Straight, square, great. Without practice, nothing is unfavorable.

Commentary on the Image: “The second six moves straight and square. Without practice, nothing is unfavorable: Earth's way shines.” Kun's virtue is upright and broad, naturally appropriate without deliberate training. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Upright, square, and broad: no contrived practice is needed. Acting from genuine honesty and correctness naturally handles matters well.

Treat people and affairs honestly and uprightly without complex strategies. Doing your proper part naturally brings good results.

Are you being sufficiently honest and upright here, or are you evading or concealing something?

Six in the third place: Concealed excellence allows steadfastness. Perhaps following the king's affairs: no personal achievement, but completion.

Commentary on the Image: “Concealed excellence allows steadfastness, emerging at the proper time. Perhaps following the king's affairs: wisdom is broad and bright.” Hold talent within and await the time, helping accomplishment rather than monopolizing it. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Keep talent within until the time to show it. Joining another's undertaking may bring no independent achievement, yet you can see it through and complete it well.

Support another person's plan or join an existing team. Do not compete for leading credit; focus on doing your own part well.

Do you currently need to set aside a desire to lead and participate as a supporter?

Six in the fourth place: Tie the sack closed. No blame and no praise.

Commentary on the Image: “Tying the sack closed brings no blame: caution prevents harm.” Close the sack's opening, remaining cautious and restrained, seeking no reputation but avoiding fault. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Stay quiet and restrained, speaking and acting little, seeking neither fame nor fault. Silence is a prudent strategy in sensitive times.

Say and do less, remain cautious, and quietly get through this period without seeking attention or displaying yourself.

What words or actions might cause trouble now? Where should you hold back temporarily?

Six in the fifth place: A yellow lower garment. Supreme good fortune.

Commentary on the Image: “A yellow lower garment brings supreme good fortune: refinement is within.” Yellow is the central color, and the lower garment signifies a supporting role. Virtue is central and correct, with refinement held within: great good fortune. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Occupy a supporting role with balanced virtue and inward refinement rather than outward display. This humble, centered state is highly auspicious.

Acting humbly and impartially in an important position, without dominating or boasting, brings very good results.

Do you retain humility and balance in your present role? Are you displaying yourself excessively or overstepping?

Six at the top: Dragons battle in the fields. Their blood is dark and yellow.

Commentary on the Image: “Dragons battle in the fields because the way has reached its limit.” Yin at its extreme tries to overtake yang's position; both fight and are harmed. The yielding way should not become excessively forceful. (Zhouyi, Commentary on the Image; public domain)

Yielding energy accumulates to its peak and tries to overstep its proper place, injuring both sides. Excessive rigidity only creates loss.

If suppressed grievances erupt at their limit, or expansion continually exceeds your proper role, both sides suffer. Learn when to speak and when to yield.

Have you suppressed any grievances for a long time? Is there an impulse to overstep that needs honest attention?

Use of the Sixes

Use of the Sixes: Lasting steadfastness is beneficial.

Kun's way is best applied through enduring right conduct. Receptive strength's greatest advantage lies in lasting constancy rather than a brief forceful burst.

When all lines are yielding yin, the key is holding the right path over time, seeking enduring stability rather than quick effects.

Quick Quiz

How is Kun related to Qian?
  • Every line is the opposite
  • Their halves are swapped
  • They differ only in the top line
View answers and explanations for every option

Every corresponding line is exactly opposite (Correct) : Correct. Qian has six yang lines and Kun six yin lines, so each corresponding position has the opposite nature.

Their halves are swapped : Exchanging upper and lower trigrams changes no line's yin or yang nature. Kun and Qian differ in nature, not position.

They differ only in the top line : Guai differs by only one line. Kun and Qian differ in all six.

Which trigram is the lower half of Kun?
  • Kun · Earth (three broken lines)
  • Gen (two broken below, one solid on top)
  • Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle)
View answers and explanations for every option

Kun · Earth (three broken lines) (Correct) : Correct. The lower three broken lines form Kun; the upper three also form Kun.

Gen (two broken below, one solid on top) : Gen's top line is solid. Kun has no solid lines anywhere.

Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle) : Kan's middle line is solid. Kun's second line is broken.

The bottom line of Kun flips to yang. Which hexagram results?
  • Fù · Returning
  • Qiān · Modesty
  • Shī · The Army
View answers and explanations for every option

Fu · Return (Correct) : Correct. The lower trigram changes from Kun to Zhen, while the upper remains Kun, forming Hexagram 24, Fu.

Qiān · Modesty : Modesty's solid line is third, with Gen below. The first line is the lowest one.

Shī · The Army : Shi · The Army has its solid line in the second position, with Kan below.
