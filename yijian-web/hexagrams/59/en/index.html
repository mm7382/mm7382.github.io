Huan · Dispersion

Let the blockage go and the space it leaves is where people return.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Huan: Success. The king approaches his temple. Crossing the great river is beneficial; steadfast correctness is beneficial.

The Tuan commentary explains the success of Huan by firmness arriving without being cornered and yielding taking its proper place outside, and says the king reaches the temple because he stands at the centre. Crossing the great water works because you ride on wood. The Xiang image is wind moving over water, with the former kings making offerings and founding temples to gather scattered hearts.

Xun above, Kan below. Wind blows across the surface of water, and the hexagram is about things coming apart. Its root sense is scattering: ice melting, ripples spreading, people drifting away. Yet the judgement grants success, because dispersal is not only loss. What should scatter is private interest, blockage and the closed circle. So the text asks for a shared centre, for the nerve to cross the great water, and for correctness. The lines move from timely rescue while the horse is still strong, through finding something to lean on, letting go of self-concern, dissolving your own faction, issuing a decision that cannot be recalled, and finally putting distance between yourself and harm. Scattering the right things gathers people better than gripping does.

The judgment opens with success and adds beneficial river crossing and steadfastness: a rare positive invitation to action amid dispersal. The lines are also mostly positive: the first's strong rescue horse brings fortune, the second regret vanishes, the third has no regret, the fourth supreme fortune, and the fifth and top no blame. None explicitly brings misfortune, danger, or regretful limitation. Yet the third's purpose turns outward, and dispersion inherently involves uncomfortable loss and separation. The overall rating is Rather Good, with benefits arising from actively letting go rather than grasping tightly. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: release the attachments that keep people apart, open up the flow of information, and rebuild a shared centre. Avoid: dissolving without regathering, or letting your important commitments wash away with everything else.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Xun above, Kan below: wind over water — a structure of dispersing.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Huan.
  • Explain why wind over water reads as dispersion.
  • Point out that Dispersion and The Well use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Huan is Kan: broken, solid, broken. The upper is Xun: a broken fourth line with two solid above. The second, fifth and sixth lines are solid.

Kan is water and Xun is wind. Wind over the surface scatters the water, so the figure reads as dispersion. Scattering is not necessarily bad: if what has collected in one place is blocked, dispersing opens it. The question in this hexagram is whether there is somewhere to regather afterwards — scattering with nowhere to return is the real loss.

A teaching example, not a prediction: an over-centralised team is split into smaller groups and communication actually speeds up — provided a shared record still exists to gather into. Huan describes that structure.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: Rescue with a strong horse. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary explains the good fortune of this first line with a single word: compliance. You move with the trend while it is still forming, rather than straining against it.

At the very start of the scattering you set out to help while the horse is still strong, and it goes well. What matters is moving with the current and moving early.

A crack in a relationship or a team is easiest to work on while everyone still has energy. Wait until people are worn out and the same words will not go in.

Is there something that would be far easier to handle now than in a month?

Nine in the second place: Amid dispersion, run to support. Regret vanishes.

The Xiang commentary says that hurrying to the support obtains what was wished for. The word here means a bench or rest that can bear your weight.

As things scatter, you move quickly to something you can lean on, and the regret disappears. The commentary reads this as finally getting the thing you wanted.

When the situation is chaotic, secure one place to stand first: a steady income, one person you trust, a fixed daily rhythm. The rest can wait.

Inside the present mess, which one thing could you steady first?

Six in the third place: Dispersing the self. No regret.

The Xiang commentary says dispersing the self is possible here because the will was already directed outward rather than inward.

You let go of your grip on your own gains and losses, so no regret follows. The reason is plain enough: what you cared about was never yourself.

Move your attention off the question of whether you are losing out and onto the work or the people, and a great deal of knotted feeling loosens by itself. This is not self-sacrifice; it is a different angle on the ledger.

Will the thing you are counting now still matter in a year?

Six in the fourth place: Dispersing one's group. Supreme good fortune. Dispersion creates a mound, beyond ordinary thought.

The Xiang commentary calls the dispersal of one's own faction brilliant and great. Breaking up the small group gathers a hill instead, a result beyond ordinary calculation.

You break up the small circle you belonged to, and this brings the highest good fortune. What gathers afterwards is a hill, an outcome most people would not have thought of.

Release the circle that only protects its own and your scope widens, with more people willing to come near. It looks like loss in the short run and reads as expansion later.

Is the circle you are guarding protecting you or confining you?

Nine in the fifth place: Dispersing a great proclamation like sweat, dispersing the king's holdings. No blame.

The Xiang commentary attributes the absence of fault to holding the correct position. A great proclamation goes out like sweat, which cannot be drawn back in, and the ruler's own store is dispersed to settle people.

You issue a major decision the way the body gives off sweat: once out, it cannot be recalled. At the same time you release what you had accumulated for yourself, and no fault attaches.

Anyone announcing a significant decision has to stop reversing it once it is said. If you are also willing to give up your own advantage first, people believe you mean it this time.

What have you given up yourself before asking others to cooperate?

Nine at the top: Dispersing blood, going far away and emerging. No blame.

The Xiang commentary states the point of this line in two words: keeping harm at a distance. The word read as blood is taken as anxiety or injury, and the departure is a far one.

You disperse the injury and walk a long way out of it, so no fault attaches. This line is simply about leaving, and leaving far enough not to be caught by the backwash.

Some environments that drain you do not need to be fought, only left. Go cleanly and go far, and do not keep the one thread that lets you be pulled back.

Is there a place you should have left long ago and have not?

Quick Quiz

Which trigram is the lower half of Huan?
  • Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle)
  • Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above)
  • Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above)
View answers and explanations for every option

Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle) (Correct) : Correct. Broken first, solid second, broken third.

Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above) : Xun is Huan's upper trigram.

Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above) : Huan's first line is broken.

What is the deciding question in Huan?
  • Whether there is somewhere to regather afterwards
  • That scattering is always bad
  • The wider the better
View answers and explanations for every option

Whether there is a place to gather again after dispersal (Correct) : Correct. Dispersion without a place of return is the real loss.

That scattering is always bad : When blocked, dispersing can restore passage.

The wider the better : The hexagram does not say that; the focus is whether gathering again is possible.

Which three lines of Huan are solid?
  • The second, fifth and sixth
  • The first, second and third
  • The fourth, fifth and sixth
View answers and explanations for every option

The second, fifth, and sixth lines (Correct) : Correct. Lower Kan's solid line is second; upper Xun's solid lines are fifth and sixth.

The first, second and third : The first and third lines are broken.

The fourth, fifth and sixth : The fourth line is broken.
