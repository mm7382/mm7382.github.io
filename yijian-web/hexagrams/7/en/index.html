64 Hexagrams / 07

Shi · The Army

Lead by discipline, and put the right people in place

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Shi: Steadfastness under a venerable leader brings good fortune and no blame.

The Tuan commentary says Shi means the multitude and correctness means the right way; whoever can keep the multitude correct can rule. The Xiang commentary sees water within the earth, and the noble person sheltering the people and gathering the many.

Shi is the image of an army and of discipline — water held deep inside the earth, strength stored within a body of people. Leading a group means moving steadily through danger on the strength of clear rules and honest conduct. The hexagram is direct about the conditions: you need character, you need discipline, and you need the right person in charge. Without a sound basis, even great force does not last.

Shi requires right conduct and the leadership of a virtuous elder for fortune without blame. Success depends on adequate leadership and discipline: with these, great undertakings are possible; without them, misfortune follows, hence the rating: Balanced.

Worth doing: define who is responsible for what, set the pace, model it yourself, and keep the group in order. Avoid: unclear instructions, uneven reward and sanction, or asking of others what you will not do.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Kun above, Kan below: danger underneath, the many on top — a structure that needs organisation.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Shi.
  • Say which line is the only solid one in Shi.
  • Work out that The Army with its halves swapped is Holding Together.

The lower trigram of Shi is Kan and the upper is Kun. Only the second line is solid; the other five are broken. That single solid line sits in the middle of the lower trigram and is the one point of leverage in the whole figure.

Kun is the many, the ground; Kan is danger. Shi puts the danger below and the many above: a large group has to move over unstable ground. With only one solid line, the point is not numbers but whether that single point of leverage can organise the crowd. Tradition discusses it through armies, which is a way of saying that organisation is not optional.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a large task force assembled at short notice: there are enough people, no settled process, and only one person who really holds the whole picture. Shi describes that structure; it does not say the group will deliver.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: The army sets out with discipline. Without proper order, misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says the army goes out under discipline; losing that discipline brings misfortune.

Any action has to start from clear rules. Where the standards are vague or ignored, the risk is serious.

Whatever you are starting or organising, set the rules and the discipline first, or the failure is already built in.

Does what you are running have clear rules, or is it being held together by instinct?

Nine in the second place: Amid the army, good fortune and no blame. The king grants commands three times.

The Xiang commentary says being at the centre of the army brings good fortune through the favour of heaven, and that threefold commission reflects care for all the territories.

Holding the central position and leading in a balanced, upright way is favourable and free of fault. Leaders with real character earn trust in both directions.

If you are the core of a team, lead honestly and evenly. You gain trust and you can carry a larger responsibility.

Are you acting with integrity in your group, and does your conduct match the trust you have been given?

Six in the third place: The army may carry corpses in wagons. Misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says the army carrying corpses home has accomplished nothing at all.

Putting the wrong person in a key role, or moving at the wrong time, can lead to heavy defeat. Undisciplined action causes losses you cannot recover.

Letting someone unsuited take it on, or taking on more than you can handle at the wrong moment, tends to end badly.

Is anyone here, including you, carrying something beyond their capability or timing?

Six in the fourth place: The army camps to the left. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says that camping to the left is without fault, because it does not depart from ordinary practice.

Knowing when to pull back, to shift ground rather than hold on by force, is no failure. Retreat here serves a steadier advance.

If the situation has turned against you, stepping back to regroup is not losing. It is buying strength for the next move.

Where do you need to pull back and regroup before pushing on?

Six in the fifth place: Game in the fields. Taking action is beneficial, without blame. The eldest son leads the army; the younger carries corpses. Persistence brings misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says the eldest son leads the army because he holds to the middle way, while sending the unsuitable means employing the wrong person.

There is a legitimate reason to act, but the capable person has to lead. Handing the main responsibility to someone secondary tends to end badly.

Have a proper reason to act, and let the genuinely capable person lead. Putting an underqualified person in the key role creates a mess that is hard to clean up.

Is the most suitable person in the most important role, or did convenience decide it?

Six at the top: The great ruler issues commands, founding states and establishing households. Do not employ petty people.

The Xiang commentary says the great ruler's commission rightly acknowledges merit, but that employing the small-minded is certain to throw the state into disorder.

When the work is done, credit should be distributed on merit. Keep people of poor character out of important positions, or disorder follows.

Share the results fairly once it is finished, and do not let someone with a character problem take a key position. That one costs you later.

Do you have a clear standard for sharing the results, and for who gets real influence afterwards?

Which is the only solid line in Shi?

The second line (Correct) : Correct. Lower Kan's solid line is its middle line, the second of the whole hexagram.

The fifth line : The fifth is the center of upper Kun and is broken. A solid fifth line forms Bi · Holding Together.

The first line : The first line is broken. A solid first line forms Fu.

Why is the point of Shi not numbers?
  • The figure has only one solid line, so there is only one point of leverage
  • Because the upper trigram is Kan
  • Because all six lines are yin
View answers and explanations for every option

Only one solid line in the hexagram gives a single point of force (Correct) : Correct. Five broken lines and one solid line concentrate strength at that one position.

Because the upper trigram is Kan : Shi's upper trigram is Kun; Kan is below.

Because all six lines are yin : Six yin lines form Kun. Shi has one solid line.

Swap the halves of Shi. Which hexagram do you get?
  • Bǐ · Holding Together
  • Qiān · Modesty
  • Kun · The Receptive
View answers and explanations for every option

Bi · Holding Together (Correct) : Correct. Kun below and Kan above form Hexagram 8, Bi. The solid line moves from the second to the fifth position.

Qiān · Modesty : Modesty has Gen below and Kun above, with a solid third line.

Kun · The Receptive : Kun has six yin lines. Exchanging positions cannot make a solid line disappear.
