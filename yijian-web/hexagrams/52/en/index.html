Gen · Keeping Still

Stop where stopping belongs, and think within your own ground.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Gen: Keeping the back still, not grasping the body. Walking in the courtyard, not seeing the people. No blame.

The Tuan commentary glosses Gen as stopping: stop when it is time to stop, move when it is time to move, and when neither motion nor stillness misses its moment, the way is bright. To still it is to stop it in its proper place. Upper and lower stand opposed and do not engage, hence not perceiving the body and walking the courtyard without seeing anyone, and no fault. The Xiang image is mountain doubled, and the noble person keeps his thoughts within his own position.

Gen above, Gen below. Two mountains stand one on the other: this is stopping. Gen is not about being motionless but about stopping in the right place, within what is properly yours. The judgement stills the attention at the back, where you cannot see yourself, so your own appetites do not pull you, and you cross the courtyard untouched by other people's affairs. The lines climb the body from the toes to the calves, the waist, the trunk and the jaw, ending in a stopping so settled it is no longer effort. Knowing where to stop matters as much as knowing how to advance.

None of the six lines declares misfortune. The first six gives no blame and beneficial lasting steadfastness, the fourth no blame, the fifth vanished regret, and the top nine generous stillness with fortune. Its Image affirms a substantial ending, and the Judgment praises movement and rest timed rightly as a bright way. Difficulties concern discomfort in the process rather than poor results: the second's unhappy heart reflects enforced stopping, and the third's torn back and danger scorching the heart reflect abrupt restraint. A stable ending requires self-restraint through a constrained process, hence the rating: Rather Good rather than the best. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: stop at the place where stopping belongs, and give body, mind and information room to settle. Avoid: chasing the same things while supposedly resting, or stretching a pause into an escape from responsibility.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Gen upon Gen: two stoppings stacked, each solid line at the top of its half.

  • Recognise the hexagram whose two halves are both Gen.
  • State how Gen and Zhen are related structurally.
  • Say that flipping the third line of Stilling gives Stripping Away.

Both halves of Gen are Gen: in each, the lower two lines are broken and the top one is solid. The solid lines of the figure are the third and sixth. It is the vertical mirror of Zhen: Zhen has its solid lines at the base of each half, Gen at the top.

Gen is a mountain, stopping. Each solid line sits at the top of its half, so the stopping force is at the summit with emptiness beneath. The hexagram is not about immobility but about knowing where to stop. Stopping in the right place is structure; stopping in the wrong one is merely being stuck — and the whole difficulty of this hexagram lies in that difference.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a team decides this release takes no more features and draws the line somewhere explicit. Gen describes that structure of knowing where to stop.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: Keeping the toes still. No blame. Lasting steadfastness is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary says stilling the toes means correctness has not yet been lost. Halting at the very first step, before the motion happens, means no fault has yet formed.

You stop at the toes, halting before the step is taken, so there is no fault, and long-term steadiness is favourable. The easiest moment to stop is the moment before you have started walking.

Calling a halt while the idea is still forming costs almost nothing; turning back after ten steps costs a great deal. Stop the moment it feels wrong, rather than waiting until you can prove you were wrong.

Is there something you know you should not start, with your foot already raised?

Six in the second place: Keeping the calves still, unable to rescue what they follow. The heart is unhappy.

The Xiang commentary says failing to rescue the one you follow comes from that person not yet yielding and listening. You want to stop it and have no purchase, so the heart is uneasy.

You stop at the calves. Calves only move as the thighs move, so you cannot rescue the one you follow, and that leaves you unhappy. Seeing the problem without the standing to change it is a heavy position.

Following someone else's decision, knowing it is unwise and unable to talk them out of it, is a genuinely frustrating place. Admitting you have no say this time and saving your effort works better than trying to drag along people who are not listening.

Whose decision are you quietly resenting, and was it ever yours to make?

Nine in the third place: Keeping the waist still, splitting the back muscles. Danger scorches the heart.

The Xiang commentary says stilling at the waist lets danger smoke the heart. Upper and lower are cut apart, the body cannot coordinate, and the peril reaches straight to the spirit.

You stop at the waist, the boundary between upper and lower, tearing the sinews of the back, and danger smoulders in the heart. Stopping at the joint that should least be stopped tears the body apart from within.

Slamming the brakes at the point where things most need to connect rips the upstream and downstream apart. What you stop should be the start of an action, not the critical handover halfway through.

Is the point where you want to call a halt the starting line or the handover?

Six in the fourth place: Keeping the body still. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says stilling the trunk means placing the work of stopping on yourself. You ask nothing of others and restrain only your own conduct, so there is no fault.

You stop at your own body, and there is no fault. Instead of judging whether other people should stop, you first hold your own actions in. This is the most practical approach and the one with the surest grip.

Rather than spending effort demanding restraint from your environment or the people in it, take care of your own share. The only thing genuinely in your hands is your own behaviour.

How much of your effort goes into managing others rather than yourself?

Six in the fifth place: Keeping the jaw still. Words have order; regret vanishes.

The Xiang commentary says stilling the jaw comes from holding the centre correctly. Restraining speech so that what is said has order is the mean in practice, so regret disappears.

You stop at the jaw, so your speech has order and regret disappears. Stilling at its highest reach turns out to be governing the mouth: not silence, but speaking in order and not speaking too much.

Most regrets come from the extra sentences. Order your words before they leave and decide whether they need saying at all; that alone saves a great deal of clearing up.

Lately, do you regret what you said or what you left unsaid?

Nine at the top: Generous stillness. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says the good fortune of generous stilling comes from ending with substance. At the furthest reach of stopping, nothing floats or wavers, and this is the steadiest resting place in the hexagram.

You stop with a settled, substantial attitude, and the line reads well. The stopping here is not grudging restraint but being genuinely at ease in the place that is yours.

Real restraint eventually stops feeling like endurance and becomes simply not reaching for the thing. Once stopping no longer feels like a loss, that steadiness starts working in your favour.

Is your self-discipline still endurance, or has it become a habit?

The third and sixth lines (Correct) : Correct. Each Gen trigram has a solid line at the top.

The first and the fourth : That is the arrangement of doubled Zhen.

The second and the fifth : That is the arrangement of doubled Kan.

What is Gen about?
  • Knowing where to stop
  • Total immobility
  • Pressing always forward

Knowing where to stop (Correct) : Correct. Stopping in the right place is structural; stopping wrongly is simply being stuck.

Total immobility : The hexagram concerns the position of stopping, not immobility itself.

Pressing always forward : That is Zhen's or Qian's direction, not Gen's.

The third line of Gen flips to yin. Which hexagram results?
  • Bo · Splitting Apart
  • Qiān · Modesty
  • Bì · Adornment
View answers and explanations for every option

Bo · Splitting Apart (Correct) : Correct. Lower Gen changes to Kun while upper Gen remains, forming Hexagram 23, Bo.

Qiān · Modesty : Modesty has Gen below and Kun above; its upper trigram is not Gen.

Bì · Adornment : Bi · Grace has Li below and Gen above, resulting from changing the first line.
