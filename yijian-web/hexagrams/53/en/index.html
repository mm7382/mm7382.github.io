Jian · Gradual Progress

Climb one rung at a time and let nothing skip its stage.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Jian: A woman's marriage brings good fortune. Steadfast correctness is beneficial.

The Tuan commentary reads Jian as advance by degrees. The advance holds its position, so going forward accomplishes something; the advance is correct, so it can set a state right. The lower trigram stills and the upper yields, so movement does not exhaust itself. The Xiang image is a tree on the mountain, growing slowly yet reaching great height, and the noble person settles into worthy character and improves the customs around him.

Xun above, Gen below. A tree grows on the mountain: this is advance by stages. The whole hexagram runs on the image of wild geese in flight, moving in order from the water's edge to the rock in the stream, the dry ground, the branch, the ridge, and at last feathers fit for ceremonial use. The stages are distinct and none can be skipped. The judgement uses a marriage completed step by step through proper form, and asks you to hold correct. Only the third line breaks away and presses ahead alone, and that is the one that goes badly. Slow is not the problem; out of sequence is.

The judgment explicitly gives good fortune with right conduct. The second six, fifth nine, and top nine have fortune, and the fourth no blame; the Image describes positive long-term cultivation of virtue and customs. Negativity is concentrated in the third nine's husband not returning and pregnancy not bearing a child. Its Image attributes this to leaving the group and losing the Way: avoidable rashness outside the proper sequence, rather than the overall theme. Conditions are favorable but require time and order, hence the rating: Rather Good rather than the best. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: build capability and relationships in sequence, and settle on each stage before moving to the next. Avoid: skipping necessary steps, pressing for commitment, or changing course because the pace feels slow.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Xun above, Gen below: a mountain beneath wind — a structure of step by step.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Jian (Gradual Progress).
  • Explain why stopping below and yielding above reads as gradual.
  • Point out that Gradual Progress and Repairing What Has Decayed use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Jian is Gen: two broken lines with a solid third. The upper is Xun: a broken fourth line with two solid above. The third, fifth and sixth lines are solid.

Gen is stopping and Xun enters by going along. With a boundary below and yielding above, each step moves forward inside the boundary, which reads as gradual. The value here is cadence: not slowness, but not taking the next step until this one is firm. Progress that skips steps does not count as progress in this structure.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a team changes one module at a time, verifies it, and only then touches the next. Jian describes that structure.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: The wild goose gradually reaches the shore. Danger for the young person; criticism, but no blame.

The Xiang commentary says the young one's danger carries no fault in principle. The risk and the gossip come from being early in a career, not from anything done wrong.

The goose has just reached the bank at the water's edge, low and not yet secure. At this stage a young person feels exposed and hears some talk, yet there is no fault in it.

Being questioned about your inexperience is normal when you first enter a field. What helps is not arguing back but doing the most basic part of the work so well that nobody has a handle on you.

Is the remark aimed at your ability, or just at how long you have been here?

Six in the second place: The wild goose gradually reaches the rock. Eating and drinking contentedly. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says eating and drinking in contentment is not being fed for nothing. The ease was earned by your own footing, so it can be enjoyed.

The goose moves on to the great rock in the stream and stands firmly, eating and drinking at ease, and the line reads well. The commentary adds that this is not something received for free but security you walked to yourself.

After getting through the hardest early stretch, you finally have solid ground and can rest for a while. Enjoy it without guilt, and remember that the rock is where you walked to, not a gift.

How did the solid ground under your feet come to be there?

Nine in the third place: The wild goose gradually reaches the plateau. The husband campaigns and does not return; the wife is pregnant but does not bear a child. Misfortune. Defending against raiders is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary says the husband who marches out and does not return has left the flock, and the wife who conceives and does not bear has lost the proper way. Only turning to defence, so that each protects the other, is favourable.

The goose jumps a stage to the dry high ground, out of its sequence. So the one who sets out does not return and the one who conceives does not bear, and the outcome is poor. Only defence suits here, not an offensive move.

Forcing progress past the steps you owe tends to lose you both ends: the new thing does not land and what you already held slips. Stop expanding and protect what you have and the people around you.

Which step did you skip, and are you paying for it now?

Six in the fourth place: The wild goose gradually reaches the tree; perhaps it finds a level branch. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says finding a flat branch is possible through a yielding, accommodating attitude. The bird bends to the situation and finds somewhere it can rest.

The goose flies up into a tree, where its webbed feet cannot grip a branch, yet it may find one flat enough to perch on for a while, so there is no fault.

When you are put somewhere that does not suit you, you need neither grit your teeth nor walk out at once. Lower your stance, find the angle the environment allows, and settle before deciding anything else.

In this ill-fitting position, which flat branch could you hold for now?

Nine in the fifth place: The wild goose gradually reaches the hill. The wife does not conceive for three years, but in the end nothing can overcome them. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says that in the end nothing overcomes it, and what is wished for is obtained. The goose reaches the high ridge and is separated for a time, yet the proper correspondence holds.

The goose advances to the high ridge, with obstruction along the way, like a woman who does not conceive for three years. Those obstacles do not finally outweigh the genuine connection, and what was hoped for arrives.

A partnership or relationship that genuinely fits can still be misread or kept apart for years. As long as neither side has gone off course, time wears the obstacle down, and nothing drastic is needed to force it back.

Is the thing you have waited a long time for simply not yet due?

Nine at the top: The wild goose gradually reaches the plateau. Its feathers can be used in ceremony. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says the feathers serve as ceremonial ornament because the formation was never disordered. This gathers the whole hexagram's theme of sequence into its closing image.

The goose reaches the highest ground, and its feathers are clean and ordered enough to be used as ceremonial ornament, so the line reads well. The commentary points to the order that was never broken.

Someone who came up by the proper route leaves behind more than results: they leave a way of doing things others can use as a standard. That influence comes from having taken no shortcuts.

If someone copied your method exactly, what would you want them to learn from it?

Quick Quiz

Which trigram is the lower half of Jian (Gradual Progress)?
  • Gen (two broken below, one solid on top)
  • Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above)
  • Kun · Earth (three broken lines)

Gen · Mountain (two broken lines below, one solid above) (Correct) : Correct. The first and second lines are broken; the third is solid.

Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above) : Xun is Jian's upper trigram.

Kun · Earth (three broken lines) : The third line is solid.

What is the point of “gradual” in Jian?
  • Not taking the next step until this one is firm
  • The slower the better
  • Steps may be skipped
View answers and explanations for every option

Make each step secure before taking the next (Correct) : Correct. It concerns rhythm, not slowness.

The slower the better : Slowness is not the point; secure footing is.

Steps may be skipped : Skipping steps does not count as progress in this structure.

Which three lines of Jian are solid?
  • The third, fifth and sixth
  • The first, second and third
  • The fourth, fifth and sixth
View answers and explanations for every option

The third, fifth, and sixth lines (Correct) : Correct. Lower Gen's solid line is third; upper Xun's solid lines are fifth and sixth.

The first, second and third : The first and second lines are broken.

The fourth, fifth and sixth : The fourth line is broken.
