Kun · Oppression

Held fast, keep your ground and let actions speak.

Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Kun: Success. Steadfastness brings the great person good fortune and no blame. Words are not believed.

The Tuan commentary says Confinement is firmness covered over: meeting danger yet staying agreeable, being confined without losing your own ground, which is what allows success and is perhaps only within reach of the noble person. Words not being believed comes from prizing talk, which only runs to a dead end. The Xiang image is a marsh with no water in it, and the noble person who will surrender life itself to carry out the will.

Dui above, Kan below. The marsh has no water: resources are exhausted and movement in every direction is blocked. What matters in Confinement is not whether you can escape but whether you hold on to yourself while you are held. The judgement says that words are not believed, so explaining yourself only makes the position more awkward. The lines run from a stump in a dark valley through confinement amid food and drink with help on its way, a place with injury ahead and thorns behind, a slow arrival held up by a fine carriage, a maiming at the top that slowly loosens, and finally entangling vines that still allow a forward step.

The name means deprivation; the Judgment says the firm is covered, and the Image's lake without water depicts exhausted resources. The third six's stone, thorns, and absent wife explicitly bring misfortune. The first's three years unseen depicts prolonged darkness; the fifth includes mutilation. The judgment adds that words are not believed, leaving little room even to explain. Although it offers success, steadfast good fortune and no blame for the great person, and the top six fortune in advancing, these are exits for those preserving integrity under pressure, not favorable circumstances themselves, hence the rating: Unfavorable. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: hold the limits that matter, conserve your strength, and let actions stand in for argument. Avoid: repeated complaint, promises made under pressure, or trading away a principle to get out.

Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Dui above, Kan below: water beneath a lake — a structure of confinement.

  • Name the upper and lower trigram of Kun (Confinement).
  • State how Kun (Confinement) differs from Jian.
  • Point out that Confinement and Limitation use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Kun is Kan: broken, solid, broken. The upper is Dui: two solid lines with a broken sixth. The second, fourth and fifth lines are solid.

Kan below is a pit and Dui above is a lake. The water underneath cannot get out, and the lake above is visible but not connected, so the figure reads as confinement. Like Jian it speaks of being unable to move, but Kun stresses resources that are right there yet unusable. This structure is easily mistaken for a lack of ability when it is a lack of a channel.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a team can see that the company already has the tool it needs but has no route to request it, so it rebuilds one. Kun describes that structure.

The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

Six at the beginning: Buttocks oppressed by a tree stump. Entering a dark valley, unseen for three years.

The Xiang commentary says that entering the dark valley is a matter of obscurity itself: the surroundings cannot be seen clearly, so the light stays out of reach for a long stretch.

Stuck on a bare stump, unable to move, then down into a deep dark valley with no one in sight for a long time. This is the floor of Confinement: not only blocked, but unable to see which way anything lies.

In a low patch with no clear direction, hold off on big decisions. Keep the basic rhythm of your days going and choose a route once you can see a little further; that usually beats moving in the dark.

In this low patch, what is the one thing you can still see clearly?

Nine in the second place: Oppressed amid wine and food. Crimson ceremonial sashes are coming. Sacrificial offerings are beneficial. Advancing brings misfortune, but no blame.

The Xiang commentary says there is still cause for gladness within this confinement because the centre is held. The scarlet knee-covers stand for a person of rank, so help is approaching and stillness suits better than haste.

Confined amid food and drink, comfortable-looking but stalled, with the scarlet robes on their way: help is in transit. Sincere observance suits, a rushed campaign does not, and holding your place carries no fault.

When a chance is approaching but not yet in hand, the worst move is to rush out and prove yourself. Finish the work in front of you and stay sincere while the other side closes the distance.

Does the opportunity you are waiting on need action from you now, or stillness?

Six in the third place: Oppressed by stone, resting on thorns. Entering the palace, not seeing one's wife. Misfortune.

The Xiang commentary attributes leaning on thorns to the yielding riding on the firm, and calls returning home to find no wife inauspicious. A boulder blocks the way forward and thorns lie behind.

A boulder blocks the advance, thorns lie underfoot on the retreat, and the person closest to you is not there when you get home. This is the most severe line in the hexagram: both directions injure, and the way back is gone too.

When you hit a wall outside and the support at home has also gone, the thing to do is stop pushing anything forward and repair the relationships still with you. Read this as a description of the situation, not a prediction.

While you have been pushing outward, who at home has quietly stepped back?

Nine in the fourth place: Coming slowly, oppressed by a metal carriage. Regret, but completion.

The Xiang commentary says the slow arrival comes from a will directed below, and that although the position is unsuitable there is still someone in support, so an outcome does come.

Movement is slow and held up by a fine metal carriage: the obstacle is not scarcity but encumbrance and hesitation. The process carries some embarrassment, yet it does reach an end.

Being unable to help the person you want to help, or reach the place you want to reach, is usually the weight of what you cannot bring yourself to put down. Slower is acceptable if the direction is right.

Is what stops you an obstacle outside, or something you will not set down?

Nine in the fifth place: Nose and feet cut off, oppressed by red ceremonial sashes. Gradually release comes. Sacrifice is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary says the maiming shows a will not yet realised, the gradual easing comes from being centred and upright, and the offering brings blessing. The imagery of mutilating punishment belongs to the period and is not a present-day instruction.

Injured while holding high office, with even the robes of rank becoming a constraint. Yet by staying centred and straight the bind slowly loosens, and a sincere, respectful attitude suits the moment best.

When someone in a senior position gets caught, the damage runs deeper and is harder to talk about. Do not expect to reverse it in one move; stay straight, work the knots loose one at a time, and time works with you.

Is part of what binds you now the standing you worked to earn?

Six at the top: Oppressed by vines and instability. Saying movement brings regret. Recognizing regret, advancing brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says that being bound by creepers shows a position that is not fitting, and that feeling the regret of movement and actually forming that regret makes going forward a favourable act.

Wrapped in creeping vines and unable to stand steady, you think that any move will be regretted. Yet it is precisely that regret that makes you willing to change, and being willing to go forward reads well.

The end of a confined stretch is often a tangle of small entanglements. Admitting the old approach was flawed and setting off in a revised direction beats standing still worrying about which step is wrong.

Which move you keep fearing to get wrong is costing you more by not being made?

Quick Quiz

Which trigram is the lower half of Kun (Confinement)?
  • Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle)
  • Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above)
  • Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle)
View answers and explanations for every option

Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle) (Correct) : Correct. Broken first, solid second, broken third.

Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above) : Dui is Kun's upper trigram.

Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle) : Li and Kan are opposites; Kun's first line is broken.

What does Kun (Confinement) stress?
  • Resources are right there and cannot be used
  • That there are no resources at all
  • A lack of ability
View answers and explanations for every option

Resources are nearby but inaccessible (Correct) : Correct. The lake above is visible, but water below cannot connect to it.

That there are no resources at all : Upper Dui has two solid lines; resources do exist.

A lack of ability : This concerns the channel, not ability.

How does Kun differ from Jian?
  • Jian is stopped underfoot; Kun has no channel
  • They share the same lower trigram
  • Kun has no solid lines
View answers and explanations for every option

Jian stops at the feet; Kun has a blocked channel (Correct) : Correct. Jian's lower trigram is Gen; Kun's is Kan.

They share the same lower trigram : Jian's lower trigram is Gen; Kun's is Kan.

Kun has no solid lines : Kun has three solid lines.
