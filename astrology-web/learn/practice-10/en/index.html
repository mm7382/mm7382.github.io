Write Your Own Summary

By the end you can turn your earlier notes into a summary in your own words, and say what each line is based on.

Approved original lessons, free on the web · 2026-10-12

THE IDEA

Your task: write your own summary, and say what every line is based on.

01

Writing the summary in four parts keeps your observations whole.

How the summary is built

Part one: what data you have, including the layer that is missing. Part two: one line for each of the three key placements. Part three: the clearest single gap. Part four: which descriptions did not fit you, and your guess at why.

Reflection prompt

Part four matters most. Being willing to write down what did not fit is what makes this your own record of observation, every line grown from your own experience.

Reflection prompt

What the old texts say: Book III ch. 13 of Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos says such judgements need many observations together, never a simple or offhand approach; Book I ch. 2 also asks that seed, place of birth, upbringing and custom be considered.

Cited source
02

After every line, mark which kind of basis it has.

A basis for every line

As you write, mark each line with one of three bases: computed (positions, aspects, degrees); traditional (things with a chapter you can look up); or my own observation (things you have really lived through).

Reflection prompt

Once you have marked them, you will notice that the useful lines are mostly the third kind, and the second kind only supplies words. If a line fits none of the three, delete it — it came from somewhere else and you are not actually sure of it.

Reflection prompt

What the old texts say: the text explains how a rule was worked out (Book I ch. 17 explains why Leo goes to the Sun, for example), and says which judgements need more conditions.

Cited source

Teaching example

Demonstration, not a real person

“My Moon has two candidates” — basis: computed.

“The Moon rules the feeling side” — basis: traditional, Book III ch. 13.

“When plans change, I phone first” — basis: my own observation.

These three lines show how to mark a basis, in a format you can copy for your own. · This is a teaching example, not your chart.

OBSERVE YOUR OWN CHART

Look at my chart

Your turn: write your own. You can bring your earlier notes over and rework them.

  1. Write the four parts: data, three lines, the gap, and what did not fit.
  2. Mark each line: computed, traditional, or my own observation.
  3. Delete any line you cannot mark.

LOOK CLOSER

A common mix-up

“A summary should be one catchy line.”

Book III ch. 13 of the Tetrabiblos asks for many observations together; writing in parts keeps the gaps and the open questions in view.

“Writing down what did not fit is embarrassing.”

That part is your own observation. The text itself says predictions sometimes fail and that more than the sky shapes a person.

CHECK YOURSELF

Now, try it yourself.

1. What do you do with a line in your summary that has no basis?

2. What must every line of the summary carry?

Limits of this lesson

A summary is a record of describing yourself through the chart, not a verdict or a prediction; every line should lead back to a basis or your own experience.

The three demonstration lines only show the format; they are not anyone's summary.
